Executive summary

There is no single winner for every purpose. Some models fit ordinary river flow best; others catch more high-flow days but raise more false alarms.

For the 10-day test

The fine-tuned encoder–decoder and CAMELS-US fine-tuned LSTM had the lowest high-flow errors in the shared comparison. The encoder–decoder caught about 80% of high-flow days. The CAMELS fine-tuned model caught about 77%, with more false alarms.

Plain-language meaning: these are promising candidates to test further, not confirmed warning systems.

For the 30-day test

The climatology-residual ensemble had the best overall day-30 score (NSE 0.858). HBV + LSTM caught the most high-flow days (recall 0.745), but its flood precision was 0.432, so many alerts were false.

Plain-language meaning: overall river tracking and flood catching are different jobs.

Three things leaders should remember

  1. The flood sample was small: 51 high-flow days in the main 2022–2024 comparison.
  2. Many tests used historical ERA5-Land weather plus artificial noise in place of archived forecast weather.
  3. “Flood day” here means above a statistical high-flow threshold. It is not an official flood warning level or an inundation record.
Decision note: the results are useful for choosing what to test next. They do not yet support issuing operational 10–30-day flood warnings.

My contribution to the project

“I built a data-to-evaluation workflow for testing river-flow forecasting at Marala, then used it to find what is promising, what is not, and what still needs stronger evidence.”

1 · Organized the evidence
Brought together observed Marala discharge records and organized them with clear source labels and model-ready daily tables. Kept model inputs, checkpoints and reports in navigable folders.
2 · Added catchment context
Prepared rainfall, temperature, snow, dewpoint, sunlight, evaporation and elevation-based inputs so tests could represent monsoon rain and snowmelt conditions.
3 · Built reusable model workflows
Set up configurable training and evaluation for 10-, 15- and 30-day forecasts, including LSTMs, transfer learning, tree methods, time-series models and physical/hybrid models.
4 · Compared models by the decision
Reported ordinary-flow fit separately from high-flow errors, flood recall and false alarms. This showed that a high overall score does not necessarily mean floods are caught.
5 · Protected target quality
Kept missing targets out of the loss in observed-target workflows and marked the experimental GRRR-filled-target run as rejected rather than presenting it as observed truth.
6 · Made results easier to inspect
Created comparison reports and a data portal that displays station inputs and source names, making it easier to review which data exists and where it came from.

What the results let me say

Several models show promise on the saved historical comparisons: transfer learning and encoder–decoder models lowered some short-range flood errors, while a climatology-residual ensemble gave the strongest saved average-flow score at day 30. HBV + LSTM caught more day-30 high-flow cases but had a substantial false-alarm burden. No single model wins every measure.

Be precise about the boundary: this contribution is a forecasting research and evaluation pipeline, plus retrospective model comparisons. Current evidence does not establish an operational flood-warning service because archived future-weather inputs and a fresh untouched test period are still needed.

Data behind these forecasts

Models learn by comparing historical weather and river conditions with observed flow. The target column is daily Marala discharge in cubic metres per second.

Training and evaluation years

StageYearsWhat the model did with them
Training2016–2020Learn model weights and fit training-only input scaling.
Validation2021Choose when to stop training and set permitted adjustments.
Test2022–2024Compare forecasts with observed targets; missing targets are skipped.
Transfer pretrainingEight CAMELS-US basins, historical periods documented in its reportSeparate donor data used only by the multi-basin transfer model.

Inputs used in the main shared LSTM table

CHIRPS precipitation, ERA5-Land rainfall, temperature, snow water equivalent, snow depth, snowmelt, dewpoint, incoming solar radiation, and potential evaporation. Future weather channels were usually precipitation and temperature. Static catchment attributes were optional and off in the main single-basin configuration. Some models additionally used past discharge, nearby/GRRR readings, elevation-band weather, or physical HBV states.

Why the test needs improving: the core Marala record is short, flood days are sparse, river flow is regulated, and old test years have already informed project decisions. A future report should include new untouched years and forecasts built only from weather information that was actually available at the time.

How to explain this to a nontechnical audience

If someone asks…Simple answer
Which model is best?For ordinary flow at 30 days, the climatology-residual ensemble scored best overall. For catching high flows at 30 days, HBV + LSTM caught the most, but generated many false alarms. For the 10-day test, fine-tuned sequence and transfer models had the best high-flow error/recall combination.
How accurate is it?There is no one accuracy number. We report how close the flow values are (RMSE/NSE) and how many high-flow days are caught (recall), separately.
Does 95% overall accuracy mean it catches 95% of floods?No. Most test days were not high-flow days. Flood recall was much lower than overall accuracy for many models.
Can it issue real 30-day warnings today?Not based on these tests alone. The experiment mostly used historical reanalysis as a future-weather stand-in, not archived operational forecasts.
Why keep the simple seasonal average?It is an honest benchmark. A complex model should beat it on new observed data before we rely on it.

Suggested next evaluation

  1. Collect additional observed discharge years and, when available, other basins.
  2. Archive real weather forecast runs with issue time and lead day.
  3. Keep a new set of years untouched until final evaluation.
  4. Publish two rankings: average-flow accuracy and flood detection.
  5. Report false alarms as well as missed floods; test the alert threshold with river managers.

“The model can help us compare possible forecasts. We still need more real-time-like tests before trusting it for flood warnings.”
